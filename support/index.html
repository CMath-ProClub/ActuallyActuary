Questions I get asked a lot

These are the questions I keep getting asked in person, and the ones I went looking for answers to myself and could not find a straight one. Answers rather than one-liners - open whichever one you came for. I am a student writing these, not a firm, and I have said so wherever it changes how much weight the answer deserves.

What does an actuary actually do?

Put a price on things that have not happened yet. An insurer has no idea which of its two hundred thousand policyholders will crash a car next year. An actuary works out what the whole book will cost, what to charge for it, and how much money the company has to hold back so it can pay the claims when they arrive.

In practice that splits into pricing (what should this cost), reserving (how much do we owe on claims that have already happened but are not settled yet), and capital modelling (how bad could a bad year get, and do we survive it). Pension and investment actuaries do a fourth thing: match a pile of assets to a stream of promises falling due over the next fifty years.

The day-to-day is far more spreadsheets, data cleaning and writing than it is exotic mathematics. The maths is the part you have to get right. The writing is the part that decides whether anybody acts on it.

Why insurance companies care about probability

Insurance sells certainty and keeps the uncertainty. That trade only works because of the law of large numbers: one person's car crash is unpredictable, but two hundred thousand people's crashes are not. The average cost per policy gets steadier as the pool grows, and that steadiness is the entire product.

So a premium is roughly the expected cost of your risk, plus expenses, plus a margin for the fact that the estimate could be wrong. Probability is not decoration on top of the business. It is the inventory.

The part people miss is that the spread matters more than the average. An insurer holds capital against the bad tail, not against the mean, because the mean year has never once bankrupted anybody.

What types of actuaries are there?

Far more than anybody tells you. Careers talks show four boxes - life, health, property and casualty, pensions - and stop, which is how people end up choosing between four options when there were thirty.

So here is the wider map. Some of these employ a few hundred people worldwide, and that is exactly why they are worth knowing about: a field small enough to be overlooked is a field where being one of the few people who understands it is itself the career. Each one says what the work is and how somebody actually gets into it.

The four you will be shown4

Every introduction to the profession stops here. They are the four largest employers of actuaries and they are a fine place to start - they are just not the whole map.

  • Life and annuitiesMortality, savings products and guarantees that run for decades.Route in SOA exams. The biggest single employer of new actuaries.
  • HealthWhat medical care will cost next year, and what a plan must charge.Route in SOA exams. Insurers, consultancies, and increasingly hospital systems.
  • Property and casualtyCar, home, liability and everything that can be damaged or sued over.Route in CAS exams. The path diverges from the SOA after Exam FM.
  • Pensions and retirementFunding promises that come due long after everybody involved retires.Route in SOA retirement track, plus an enrolled actuary designation in the US.
Lines of business most people never hear of11

Every one of these is a real actuarial team somewhere, and several are small enough that being one of the few people who understands them is a career in itself.

  • CyberPricing ransomware, breach and business-interruption cover with barely a decade of loss history and a threat that rewrites itself yearly. The hardest pricing problem in insurance right now.Route in P&C specialty teams, Lloyd's syndicates and cyber-native MGAs.
  • Crop and agricultureDrought, hail and yield shortfalls, partly underwritten by government programmes. Weather and satellite data rather than driving records.Route in A handful of specialist insurers, plus the USDA Risk Management Agency.
  • Title insuranceCover against somebody turning up with a better claim to a property than the person who sold it to you. Almost all the loss happens before the policy is written, which inverts the usual problem.Route in A small number of title insurers, mostly US.
  • Warranty and service contractsThe extended warranty on an appliance, a phone or a car. Enormous volume, tiny individual claims, and a business built on how people behave rather than how things break.Route in Manufacturers, retailers and specialist warranty administrators.
  • SuretyBonds guaranteeing a contractor finishes the job. Closer to credit underwriting than to insurance.Route in Commercial P&C insurers with a surety division.
  • Marine, aviation and spaceHulls, cargo, airlines and satellites. Very few risks, each enormous, which makes credibility theory the daily problem rather than a topic from Exam C.Route in Lloyd's, London market, and the large specialty reinsurers.
  • PetOne of the fastest-growing lines in personal insurance, with claims behaviour that looks more like health insurance than like property.Route in Pet-specific insurers and MGAs; a young field with young teams.
  • Terrorism and political riskCover backed in part by government schemes, priced without anything resembling a credible claims history.Route in Specialty reinsurers and government-backed pools.
  • CaptivesA large company insuring itself through an insurer it owns. You end up advising the parent business as much as underwriting.Route in Captive managers in Vermont, Bermuda and Cayman.
  • TakafulIslamic cooperative insurance, structured to comply with Sharia. A genuinely different model, not a relabelled one.Route in Major markets in Malaysia, the Gulf and Indonesia.
  • Mortgage and creditInsuring a lender against a borrower defaulting. Correlated, cyclical, and the reason a lot of people learned what 2008 meant.Route in Mortgage insurers and the credit risk desks of banks.
Public sector and oversight5

Actuaries who check other actuaries, or who work on numbers with no commercial owner at all. Pay is below the private market and the work is unusually consequential.

  • Social insuranceThe Social Security and Medicare trust fund projections - the ones quoted in every argument about the programmes' future. A handful of people produce them.Route in SSA and CMS Offices of the Chief Actuary.
  • State insurance regulatorReviewing the rate filings insurers submit, and deciding whether an increase is justified. You see every company's homework.Route in State departments of insurance; a common route back into industry.
  • Pension guaranteeValuing and taking over pension plans whose sponsors have failed.Route in The PBGC in the US, and its equivalents elsewhere.
  • Rating agenciesJudging whether an insurer can pay its claims. An A.M. Best or S&P rating decides who an insurer is allowed to do business with.Route in A.M. Best, S&P, Moody's, Fitch.
  • Medicaid and public programmesSetting the rates states pay managed care organisations. Directly decides what care gets funded.Route in State agencies and the consultancies they hire.
Finance-adjacent5

Where the actuarial toolkit gets pointed at capital markets. The highest ceilings in the profession sit in this group, and so does the most career risk.

  • Insurance-linked securitiesTurning catastrophe risk into a tradeable security. Cat bonds, collateralised reinsurance, sidecars.Route in Reinsurers, ILS funds and the Bermuda market.
  • Longevity and life settlementsTrading the risk that people live longer than expected, and valuing existing life policies as assets.Route in Reinsurers, pension risk transfer desks and specialist funds.
  • Bank stress testingRegulatory capital and loss forecasting under CCAR and IFRS 9. Nearly the same mathematics as reserving, aimed at loans.Route in Bank risk functions, which recruit actuaries deliberately.
  • Insurance mergers and appraisalValuing a block of business or a whole insurer for a sale.Route in Consultancies, investment banks and insurer corporate development.
  • Risk outside insuranceEnergy trading, airline fuel hedging, utilities. Anywhere a business carries a tail it does not understand.Route in ERM teams in non-financial companies. Rare, and rarely advertised.
Not an insurance job at all7

Roles where the credential is the qualification but the employer has nothing to do with insurance.

  • Expert witness and litigation supportCalculating lost lifetime earnings, the cost of an injury, or damages in a wrongful death case, then defending the number in court.Route in Forensic economics practices; usually a second career after credentialing.
  • Actuarial softwareBuilding the valuation and modelling platforms every insurer runs on. Half actuary, half engineer.Route in Vendors like Moody's, Milliman, FIS and Prophet.
  • Catastrophe modellingHurricane, wildfire, flood and earthquake risk. Half actuarial and half physical science, and climate has made it one of the scarcest skills in the industry.Route in Verisk, Moody's RMS, and in-house cat teams at reinsurers.
  • Health economicsWhether a drug or treatment is worth what it costs, on data that never quite answers the question.Route in Pharmaceutical companies and HEOR consultancies.
  • InsurTechPricing at a company that has no historical book to price from, which is a genuinely different problem to the one exams prepare you for.Route in Startups and MGAs; more equity, less certainty.
  • Academia and researchTeaching the exams, or research at the SOA, CAS or a university.Route in Usually a PhD alongside or instead of fellowship.
  • Climate and ESGTranslating climate scenarios into financial exposure for regulators and boards who now have to disclose it.Route in Consultancies, regulators and large insurers. Expanding quickly.

Two structural notes. In the United States the Society of Actuaries covers life, health, retirement and investment, and the Casualty Actuarial Society covers property and casualty. They share the first two exams, P and FM, and diverge after that, so it is worth choosing on purpose rather than by drift.

And the credential travels further than the industry does. Roughly the last two groups above are jobs where being an actuary is the qualification but insurance is not the employer - which is worth knowing before you decide the profession is narrow.

I am a student, not a compensation analyst, and quoting my own salary would be quoting zero.

Starting pay barely varies by practice area. An entry-level analyst with one or two exams is roughly sixty-five to eighty-five thousand almost anywhere - which lines up with the BLS tenth percentile of $75,240, since that decile is mostly people early in the exam sequence. Most employers add a raise for each exam passed on top of it, plus paid study hours and exam fees. The areas separate later, and they separate a long way.

Treat the ordering and the spread as the useful part and the exact figures as indicative. Ceilings are chief actuary, principal or partner roles reached by a minority - they show how far a track runs, not what to expect.

Read the last column first. The bands overlap heavily in the middle and diverge at the top, which is the part that actually decides a career.

Pensions and traditional life have the most compressed ceilings. Defined-benefit plans have been closing for thirty years, so pension work is steady rather than expanding, and much of life insurance is mature, heavily regulated and slow-moving. Good, stable work; a flatter curve.

Property and casualty pays more at nearly every level, largely because credentialed casualty actuaries are scarcer and the work reprices constantly. Health sits close behind and health consulting in particular runs hot, because medical cost trend is the single most expensive uncertainty in American business.

The highest ceilings sit outside the traditional insurer path altogether: reinsurance, insurance-linked securities, catastrophe modelling and capital markets. That is where compensation stops being a salary band and starts being tied to underwriting results, so it swings hard in both directions. Bermuda reinsurance is the extreme version of this.

Employer type moves the number as much as practice area does. Consulting usually starts a little above an insurer, climbs faster, and tops out far higher for anyone who reaches principal or partner - paid for in hours, travel and having to sell work as well as do it. An insurer is the reverse trade: a flatter curve for a much more predictable life.

Location matters too. New York, Hartford, Boston, Chicago and the Bay Area run meaningfully above the national bands, and remote work has narrowed that gap without closing it.

None of that is granular enough to plan around, though, because the seats inside one practice area are not interchangeable. Reserving and machine learning sit in the same property and casualty department and are a hundred thousand dollars apart at the top. Open an area:

The common thread is that each sits on a boundary: ALM between actuarial and investment, catastrophe modelling between actuarial and physical science, predictive modelling between actuarial and software. Pay follows the boundary, not the job title.

The honest caveat on all of it: the path is slow at the start. Most people take somewhere between four and eight years to finish the exams while working full time, and the large numbers in the last two columns are ten to twenty years out, not five. The BLS median of $125,770 is the number to keep in your head - it describes the middle of the profession, and most people spend most of their career near it rather than near a ceiling.

What is credibility?

Credibility is the formal answer to how much you should trust your own data. Say a small town has forty car insurance policies and three claims last year. Is the town genuinely risky, or did three unlucky things happen to forty people?

Credibility theory answers with a weight, Z, between zero and one. Your estimate becomes Z times what your own data says, plus one minus Z times what a bigger and more general pool says. More data pushes Z toward one and lets your own experience speak for itself. Thin data pushes Z toward zero and leans on the broader pool.

The classical version sets a full-credibility standard - a claim count large enough for a chosen accuracy - and takes Z as the square root of your count divided by that standard. Buhlmann credibility derives the weight instead from how much of the variation sits between groups rather than within them, which is the more satisfying construction.

It is the actuarial form of a habit worth having everywhere: do not over-react to a small sample. It is the same instinct that makes me distrust a backtest with twelve trades in it.

Why your car insurance premium changed

Usually not for one reason, and usually not because of anything you did.

The most common cause is that the whole book got more expensive. Parts and labour cost more, cars carry more electronics to break, medical costs rise, and crashes have become more severe. When that happens the insurer files a rate change that moves nearly everybody at once.

The second cause is your own rating factors shifting: a birthday, a new address, a different vehicle, a claim or violation ageing onto or off your record, or a discount quietly expiring.

The third is that the insurer re-filed its rating plan and now weighs the same facts about you differently. Worth knowing: in the United States that filing goes to a state regulator with an actuary's justification attached. Somebody had to argue on paper that the new number was supportable.

Actuary vs. data scientist

The overlap is real. Both build models from data, both live in statistics, and both increasingly work in Python and SQL rather than only in Excel.

The difference is the credential and what it obliges you to do. The actuarial path is a fixed exam sequence attached to a professional body, and credentialed actuaries sign opinions that regulators rely on. That makes the work conservative by design: it has to be defensible to somebody reading it years later, which rules out a model nobody can explain.

Data science is broader, faster and less standardised. No exams, no signature, and far more variety in what the job actually is. Actuaries tend to go very deep on one domain with long-horizon, low-frequency data. Data scientists more often go wide across domains with large, fast-moving data.

Pay lands in a similar place. The actuarial route is slower to start and much more predictable once you are on it.

How I built a backtester that tries to prove me wrong

It runs a trading strategy over historical price data and reports what would have happened. Strategies are written as rules in a config file rather than as code, so changing an idea does not mean changing the program.

Simulating the trades was the easy half. The hard half was stopping the thing from flattering me. A backtest that only ever agrees with you is not a test, and almost every way of building one quietly cheats.

So a signal generated on one bar fills at the opening price of the next one, never the same bar, and two tests truncate the data and check that past trades come out bit-identical. When a stop loss and a profit target both sit inside a single bar's range, the data cannot tell you which was hit first, so it takes the stop - the pessimistic reading. Every fill pays slippage, half the spread and commission.

Every result is measured against buy-and-hold and against a fixed basket of ten index funds chosen in advance and never reordered, because choosing your benchmark after seeing your result is how a losing strategy gets dressed up as a winner. Under about thirty trades it says outright that the numbers do not mean much yet. Walk-forward testing fits the parameters on one window and tests them on the next, and if efficiency falls below roughly half, it was fitting noise.

There are 280+ automated tests, and most of them exist to catch me rather than to catch a bug.

What expected value actually means

It is the long-run average, not the outcome you should expect. The expected value of one roll of a fair die is 3.5, which is a face the die does not have. Nobody has ever rolled a 3.5.

That gap matters. Expected value on its own does not tell you whether something is a good idea, because it says nothing about the spread. A bet with positive expected value that can wipe you out before the long run arrives is not a good bet. The long run is only available to people who are still solvent when it turns up.

It is also the cleanest explanation of why insurance is rational for both sides at once. The insurer holds thousands of policies and sits close to the expected value. You hold one house and do not. You are paying a small, certain amount to step away from a tail you could not absorb, and the insurer is paid to hold a tail it can. Same numbers, opposite positions, both parties behaving sensibly.
